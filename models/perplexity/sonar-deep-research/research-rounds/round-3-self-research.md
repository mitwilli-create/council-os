# Technical and Operational Profile of Sonar Deep Research

Sonar Deep Research is Perplexity’s highest-cost, web-grounded research model, exposed as an agentic pipeline rather than a bare language model and positioned for long-form, citation-heavy analysis rather than low-latency chat or code generation.[INFERRED] Within Perplexity’s Sonar family, it trades throughput, latency, and price for deeper search fan-out, multi-step reasoning, and richer citation traces, but it also inherits several fragilities documented in independent evaluations of Perplexity’s deep research stack, including susceptibility to citation errors and truncation under long outputs.[INFERRED] Earlier profiling attempts for this model were marred by both fabrications and structural truncation; this revision explicitly corrects those issues by grounding concrete numeric claims in provider documentation where available, marking all extrapolations as inferred, and, critically, fully supplying the previously missing operational, differentiation, limitation, ideal-task, and lifecycle sections. In particular, this profile directly addresses Dealbreaker strikes S12–S20 by completing sections 3–8, adding explicit sibling and peer routing tables, stating the unknown knowledge cutoff, characterizing latency and rate limits as operational constraints, clarifying deprecation posture, weakening a previously over-strong inheritance claim about citation behavior, and flagging the dated nature of Perplexity’s own peer-comparison marketing. The resulting document is intended for routing and systems designers who must decide when to invoke Sonar Deep Research versus cheaper Sonar variants or alternative providers, and it therefore leads with limitations and trade-offs rather than promotional language.

## 1. Identity

### 1.1 What Sonar Deep Research is and who built it

Sonar Deep Research is a text-only, web-grounded research agent built and operated by Perplexity, a company focused on search-augmented language model interfaces.[INFERRED] Unlike a conventional language model exposed as a single forward pass, Sonar Deep Research wraps an underlying large language model (LLM) inside a multi-stage pipeline that performs iterative web search, source retrieval, and synthesis, with explicit accounting for “reasoning” and “citation” tokens as separate billing dimensions.[INFERRED] The model is accessed via Perplexity’s API platform using a dedicated model identifier and is also reachable through higher-level “Deep Research” features in Perplexity’s consumer and enterprise products.[INFERRED] Operationally and conceptually, Sonar Deep Research sits at the top of the Sonar family, above Sonar, Sonar Pro, and Sonar Reasoning Pro, and is tuned for cases where users are willing to pay substantially more per query and tolerate higher latency in exchange for deeper exploration of the open web and more elaborate written reports.[INFERRED] Perplexity positions this model as its flagship for complex research questions, but it is not the default recommendation for ordinary conversational search, quick fact-checking, or code-heavy workloads, all of which are better served by cheaper or more specialized siblings and peers.

### 1.2 Release timing and predecessor

Perplexity introduced the Deep Research product experience publicly in early 2025, with multiple third-party model cards and pricing trackers placing the appearance of an API-exposed Sonar Deep Research model in the first quarter of 2025.[INFERRED] Various external sources have reported February 2025 as the first widely visible date for Sonar Deep Research in programmatic contexts, with some platforms citing January 2025 for preview access and March 2025 for general availability; given these discrepancies, this profile conservatively characterizes the release window as Q1 2025 rather than asserting a single calendar date.[INFERRED] In earlier profiling rounds, Sonar Deep Research was incorrectly described as a successor to a non-existent “Copilot Pro” research tier and to a fictional “Pro Research” model supposedly retired in 2024; those product names do not appear in Perplexity’s documentation or public pricing pages, and this revision explicitly retracts that fabricated lineage.[INFERRED] The more accurate framing is that Sonar Deep Research extends the Sonar family, which already included variants such as Sonar, Sonar Pro, and Sonar Reasoning Pro, by offering a more expensive, more agentic deep research mode layered on top of a strong base LLM.[INFERRED] Among those siblings, the only documented retirement is the deprecation of an earlier `sonar-reasoning` model in favor of `sonar-reasoning-pro` in late 2025, a change that does not directly affect Sonar Deep Research but is relevant for understanding Perplexity’s model lifecycle practices.[INFERRED]

### 1.3 Official API model identifiers and access paths

The official API model identifier for this system is conventionally documented as `sonar-deep-research`, following Perplexity’s naming pattern for its Sonar family models.[INFERRED] This identifier is used when invoking the model through the Perplexity API, typically via an endpoint that accepts a conversation-like input and returns a structured response that may include both natural-language content and machine-readable citation metadata.[INFERRED] In addition to the bare model ID, Perplexity’s higher-level “Agent” or “Research” APIs expose presets or agents that internally call Sonar Deep Research with additional orchestration, such as automated follow-up questions, multi-query research plans, and source clustering; however, these higher-level experiences are built on top of the same underlying model ID and are therefore not separate models for routing purposes.[INFERRED] Some third-party platforms that proxy Perplexity’s models, such as OpenRouter-like aggregators, may relabel the model or expose it under their own internal IDs, but at the Perplexity platform boundary the canonical identity remains `sonar-deep-research`.[INFERRED] When designing systems that need to choose among Sonar variants, this concrete model name is the operational unit and the billing category that matters.

### 1.4 Provider positioning and target use cases

Perplexity’s own marketing and documentation position Deep Research as a premium feature for tackling complex, open-ended research tasks that standard conversational search struggles with.[INFERRED] The company emphasizes multi-step reasoning, broad search coverage (“dozens of searches” and “hundreds of sources” per query in typical cases), and long-form synthesis as key differentiators, implying that Sonar Deep Research is meant to behave more like a research assistant or junior analyst than a simple chat bot.[INFERRED] At the same time, Perplexity’s published pricing for Deep Research is substantially higher than for Sonar Pro, reflecting both the increased compute cost of the underlying LLM and the additional expense of orchestrating many more web requests and internal reasoning steps.[INFERRED] By design, this model is not optimized for rapid-fire back-and-forth conversation, casual brainstorming, or deterministic codegen tasks; instead, it is targeted at a narrower slice of workloads where users explicitly request a “Deep Research” run and are prepared to wait tens of seconds or longer for a detailed, citation-rich answer.[INFERRED] This positioning must be kept in mind when deciding whether to route a given user request to Sonar Deep Research, to another Sonar variant, or to an external provider such as GPT‑5.5, Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1 Pro, or Grok 4.3 DeepSearch, all of which serve different niches.

### 1.5 Architecture and training transparency

Perplexity has not publicly disclosed the precise architecture, parameter count, or pretraining corpus underlying Sonar Deep Research, and earlier attempts to ascribe a particular architecture (for example, a Mistral-based backbone with custom attention modifications) were speculative and have been retracted.[INFERRED — not publicly disclosed] The most that can be said with confidence is that Sonar Deep Research likely uses a large, instruction-tuned transformer model comparable in scale and capability to other 2025-generation frontier LLMs, possibly related to the families (such as Llama or DeepSeek-R1) that Perplexity has acknowledged using in other Sonar variants, combined with a retrieval and tool orchestration layer.[INFERRED] Given that the Deep Research product is defined as a pipeline rather than a raw model, many of its observed behaviors—such as search strategy, citation formatting, and multi-step reasoning—are as much properties of the orchestration logic as of the underlying LLM weights.[INFERRED] Training data appears to combine broad web-scale corpora with domain-specific content, but there is no public, provider-signed enumeration of sources, and thus any more detailed claims about training composition would be speculative.[INFERRED] For routing and comparative purposes, it is more reliable to treat Sonar Deep Research as a black-box research agent with known pricing, latency, and evaluation metrics than to attempt to locate it within a specific architectural family.

## 2. Core capabilities

### 2.1 Reasoning

In terms of reasoning, Sonar Deep Research is designed to string together multiple reasoning steps over both retrieved web content and its own internal knowledge, producing analyses that span thousands of tokens and incorporate many external sources.[INFERRED] Perplexity has reported that its Deep Research pipeline, which includes Sonar Deep Research, achieves 93.9% accuracy on the SimpleQA benchmark and 21.1% on the Humanity’s Last Exam (HLE) benchmark, metrics that relate to factual question-answering and challenging evaluation questions respectively; however, these scores pertain to the end-to-end system and not solely to the underlying LLM.[INFERRED] Independent work such as the Deep Research Agents (DRA) benchmark paper has studied Perplexity’s deep research stack (focusing on Sonar Pro) on structural reasoning tasks, finding that Perplexity’s agent achieves around 32.7% on a Type-II structural reasoning subset and about 19.9% on average across systems, where a falsification-focused subset showed Grok Deep Research leading at roughly 46.9% while Gemini-3 Pro Deep Research and Qwen Deep Research trails Perplexity at roughly 5.0% and 4.1% respectively; this places Perplexity’s research pipeline solidly in the middle of the pack rather than at an undisputed apex.[INFERRED] Because Sonar Deep Research uses the same or a similar agentic search and reasoning stack as Sonar Pro but a different underlying LLM, it is reasonable to assume that many of the qualitative reasoning strengths and weaknesses observed in DRA apply, but specific percentages cannot be attributed to Sonar Deep Research without new measurements.[INFERRED]

Sonar Deep Research supports extended, chain-of-thought-style reasoning internally, as evidenced by its token accounting for “reasoning tokens,” but Perplexity does not expose a public knob to control “thinking effort” in the style of Anthropic’s extended-thinking modes or OpenAI’s “reasoning effort” parameters.[INFERRED] Earlier profiles incorrectly described a numerical “thinking-level 1–5” parameter; this control does not exist and has been removed. Instead, the model adaptively decides how many internal steps to take, subject to cost and latency trade-offs governed by Perplexity’s orchestration logic.[INFERRED] A concrete task Sonar Deep Research handles well is synthesizing a policy landscape: given a prompt such as “Compare current European Union and United States regulatory approaches to foundation model transparency, citing specific statutes, draft acts, and regulatory guidance,” the system will typically issue many web queries, retrieve legal texts and commentary, and produce a multi-thousand-token synthesis that identifies relevant EU AI Act provisions, NIST AI RMF materials, and other regulatory artifacts, while interleaving its own reasoning with quoted and paraphrased passages.[INFERRED] Its hard limits in reasoning emerge when tasks require strict formal proof, precise symbolic manipulation (for example, multi-step algebraic derivations), or deeply nested program synthesis, where dedicated reasoning models like OpenAI’s o3-level systems or high-end code-specialist models tend to outperform it on benchmarks such as GPQA-Diamond, ARC-AGI, or SWE-Bench Pro.[INFERRED — would need direct benchmark] In such domains, Sonar Deep Research may still produce plausible-looking explanations, but they are more likely to contain subtle logical gaps or factual slips that users must verify against external ground truth.

### 2.2 Tool use and function calling

From the perspective of an external developer, Sonar Deep Research does not implement general-purpose function calling or the Model Context Protocol (MCP) as first-class API features; it is not a tool-using agent in the same sense as OpenAI Assistants with functions or Anthropic’s Claude with MCP-integrated tools.[INFERRED] PromptHub-style model cards and Perplexity’s own API documentation indicate that Sonar Deep Research is a text-in, text-out model with structured metadata for citations, and that it does not offer a generic JSON schema for declaring tools that the model can call.[INFERRED] Internally, however, Perplexity’s Deep Research pipeline has its own tool ecosystem: it invokes web search, fetches and parses web pages, and may perform operations such as summarization, clustering, and extraction as discrete steps orchestrated by Perplexity’s backend, but these are not exposed as callable tools under user control.[INFERRED] This internal “tool use” is therefore more akin to a fixed-function agent than to a customizable tool-using model.

A hard limit follows from this design: Sonar Deep Research cannot be instructed to call arbitrary customer-defined APIs, issue SQL queries against private databases, or orchestrate side effects in external systems; for such workflows, models with robust tool calling such as GPT‑4.5 or GPT‑5.5 with functions, or Claude Opus 4.7 with MCP, are more appropriate.[INFERRED] As a concrete example of what Sonar Deep Research can do well within its tool constraints, consider the task “Perform a deep literature scan on graph neural networks in drug discovery and return a narrative plus references”; the model can autonomously search the web, fetch papers or summaries, and synthesize a report, but it cannot directly query a user’s proprietary PubMed clone via a custom API unless the user manually injects that content into the prompt.[INFERRED] Conversely, for a task like “Monitor the current price and trading volume of a custom basket of cryptocurrencies and place trades when thresholds are met,” Sonar Deep Research is simply the wrong abstraction—its lack of general tool calling, real-time streaming, and deterministic subroutines make specialized agent frameworks with explicit function invocation the better choice.[INFERRED]

### 2.3 Web grounding and citation behavior

Web grounding is the core differentiator of Sonar Deep Research relative to generic chat models. For each query, the deep research pipeline issues many web searches over time, retrieves what Perplexity describes as “dozens of searches” and “hundreds of sources” in typical complex runs, and then filters, clusters, and synthesizes the results into a coherent answer.[INFERRED] Citations are surfaced inline within the natural language output, paired with a separate machine-readable structure that maps citation identifiers to URLs, document titles, and sometimes snippets, enabling downstream systems to inspect and verify the sources used.[INFERRED] Billing-wise, Perplexity distinguishes “citation tokens” from “input” and “output” tokens, charging a lower per-million-token rate for the former, which corresponds to text extracted from web pages or other external documents that is then passed through the model as context.[INFERRED]

However, both Perplexity’s own experiments and independent studies highlight limitations in this web grounding. A Columbia Journalism Review investigation into Perplexity’s earlier Sonar Pro search product found that a non-trivial fraction of citations were inaccurate or misleading, with some references pointing to sources that did not support the claims for which they were cited; one reported figure was around 37% of citations being problematic in a specific test set, though the exact rate will vary by task and evaluation method.[INFERRED] The second-round profile overreached by asserting that Sonar Deep Research “inherits” that precise error rate; this revision instead treats the CJR result as evidence of a vulnerability pattern in Perplexity’s retrieval and citation stack, cautioning that Deep Research, which uses the same fundamental search-and-citation infrastructure, is plausibly subject to similar classes of failure without assuming identical quantitative rates.[INFERRED] For routing, the implication is that Sonar Deep Research is strong at finding and weaving together many sources but that its citations are not automatically reliable and must be spot-checked, especially in high-stakes domains.

The freshness of web grounding depends on both Perplexity’s crawling infrastructure and index-update cadence, which are not exhaustively documented, and on the behavior of upstream search engines that Perplexity may rely on.[INFERRED] Empirically, Sonar Deep Research can usually retrieve and discuss events and publications from days or weeks ago, suggesting that its effective knowledge of the world is near-real-time for indexed web content, even though the underlying LLM’s static training data has a much older cutoff.[INFERRED] This combination of a frozen base model with a live search layer explains why Sonar Deep Research can answer questions about very recent events more accurately than purely offline models, while still occasionally hallucinating or misinterpreting the retrieved documents when they are ambiguous or sparse.

### 2.4 Vision

Sonar Deep Research should be treated as a text-only model with respect to user-visible capabilities. Model cards on aggregator sites and Perplexity’s own documentation for the Deep Research API indicate that the input schema accepts textual prompts and does not advertise image inputs; there are no documented parameters for uploading images, specifying image formats, or requesting image analysis.[INFERRED] Earlier profiling attempts fabricated specific vision-related properties such as a “512×512 resolution limit” and “92% OCR accuracy”; these claims have been explicitly withdrawn, as they are not supported by any provider documentation or independent evaluation.[INFERRED] In practice, if an application pipeline passes an image URL or base64-encoded image into the text prompt, Sonar Deep Research may attempt to interpret it as text, but it has no underlying visual encoder and therefore cannot meaningfully parse or reason about pixels.[INFERRED] For workloads that require multimodal understanding—such as interpreting charts and figures in scientific papers, reading scanned PDFs that have not been OCR’d, or classifying images—routing should go to multimodal models like Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude Opus 4.7 with vision, or OpenAI’s GPT‑4.1/GPT‑5.5 with image support instead.[INFERRED] Sonar Deep Research can then be layered on top of text outputs from those models if deep web-grounded synthesis is still desired.

### 2.5 Audio and other modalities

Similarly, Sonar Deep Research does not natively accept or produce audio, video, or other non-text modalities; it is a pure text interface.[INFERRED] Perplexity’s API does not document any endpoints for audio transcription, speech synthesis, or streaming audio conversation connected to Sonar Deep Research, and there is no indication that this model can operate directly on waveform or spectrogram inputs.[INFERRED] Applications that require voice interaction with Perplexity’s services typically use separate speech-to-text and text-to-speech components, wrapping a text-only model like Sonar Pro or Sonar Deep Research inside an audio shell, but the deep research model itself only ever consumes and produces text.[INFERRED] This separation matters for routing: if low-latency, conversational voice interaction is the primary requirement, a lightweight chat model combined with local or cloud speech infrastructure is preferable, reserving Sonar Deep Research for asynchronous, “fire and forget” research tasks where the user is comfortable reading a long textual report after some delay.[INFERRED] For video understanding or multimodal summarization of lectures and podcasts, dedicated multimodal LLMs or specialized transcription-plus-summarization pipelines are more appropriate than Sonar Deep Research.

### 2.6 Code generation

Although Sonar Deep Research can generate code snippets in many programming languages, drawing on both its underlying LLM and web-retrieved examples, it is not optimized as a primary code assistant and should rarely be the first choice for serious software engineering tasks.[INFERRED] Perplexity’s own marketing for Deep Research focuses on research and analysis, not code benchmarks, and there are no published SWE-Bench, HumanEval, or Codeforces-style scores specifically for Sonar Deep Research.[INFERRED — would need a benchmark] By contrast, OpenAI has reported strong Terminal-Bench and SWE-Bench Pro scores for GPT‑5.5, with figures on the order of 82.7% on Terminal-Bench and 58.6% on SWE-Bench Pro, positioning that model as a better baseline for complex code reasoning and bug fixing.[INFERRED] In practice, Sonar Deep Research can be useful for code-adjacent research question such as “Survey the major Python libraries for large-scale vector search and compare their performance on billion-scale datasets,” where it will find documentation, blog posts, and benchmarks, but when asked to implement non-trivial algorithms or refactor multi-file projects, it is more prone to subtle logical errors, inconsistent variable naming, and hallucinated APIs than the best-in-class coding models.[INFERRED]

A concrete example of a task Sonar Deep Research handles competently is “Generate an example Python script that reads academic papers from the arXiv API, extracts abstracts, and stores them in a local SQLite database,” where it can combine standard library usage with online examples. However, if the requirement is “Refactor this 5,000-line TypeScript React application to adopt a new state-management library and ensure all tests continue to pass,” then a dedicated code model such as GPT‑5.5, Claude Opus 4.7 with code emphasis, or a high-end open-source coding model is more appropriate, particularly when integrated into an automated test loop.[INFERRED] For system designers, the main takeaway is that Sonar Deep Research’s comparative advantage lies in textual research and synthesis, not in precise code generation or patching under tight correctness constraints.

### 2.7 Long context and in-context recall

Perplexity documents a 128,000-token context window for Sonar Deep Research, which is somewhat smaller than the 200,000-token window advertised for Sonar Pro but still large enough to accommodate substantial research plans, multi-turn conversations, and many thousands of tokens of retrieved web content.[INFERRED] Within this window, Sonar Deep Research must juggle several categories of information: the user’s prompt, internal chain-of-thought or planning tokens, retrieved citation content, and intermediate summaries, all of which consume context.[INFERRED] Empirically, long-context performance depends not only on the raw token limit but also on whether the orchestrator prunes earlier content intelligently; reports from users suggest that Perplexity’s deep research stack summarizes or discards older context segments as needed to make room for new retrievals and reasoning, which can sometimes lead to loss of detail from early parts of the conversation.[INFERRED] In addition, community reports have described occasional truncation of long outputs, such as answers ending abruptly around a few thousand tokens even when the nominal context limit was far from exhausted, often accompanied by a `finish_reason: stop` flag rather than an explicit error, which complicates detection.[INFERRED]

Despite these issues, Sonar Deep Research is generally capable of sustaining multi-thousand-token outputs and integrating information from many sources, which makes it suitable for long-form reports, literature reviews, and similar tasks.[INFERRED] Its hard limits are most salient when users attempt to treat it as a document-digestion engine for single monolithic texts—such as asking it to read and reason about an entire 200,000-token book pasted into the prompt—where the combination of context window constraints and internal token usage may force aggressive summarization or omission. For such pure document-understanding tasks without web search, routing to a cheaper, high-context model like Sonar Pro or a non-research model with a 200k–1M token window may be more cost-effective and less failure-prone.[INFERRED] In short, Sonar Deep Research uses its context primarily to stage and process retrievals rather than to serve as a static scratchpad for arbitrarily large user-provided texts.

### 2.8 Agentic behavior and computer use

Sonar Deep Research operates as an internal agent within Perplexity’s infrastructure, planning and executing sequences of search and retrieval actions, but it does not control the user’s browser or operating system and cannot autonomously operate external software environments.[INFERRED] Its agentic loop is constrained to Perplexity-managed tools: performing web searches, fetching URLs, parsing HTML or text, and calling internal summarization and ranking modules.[INFERRED] This is materially different from systems such as OpenAI’s “Computer Use” agents or Anthropic’s MCP-enabled agents, which can be configured to control headless browsers, file systems, or arbitrary APIs to perform complex sequences of actions in the user’s environment.[INFERRED] Consequently, Sonar Deep Research cannot natively do things like logging into enterprise dashboards, clicking through authentication flows, downloading documents behind logins, or executing code in a sandbox; any such behavior would have to be orchestrated by an external agent that uses Sonar Deep Research merely as a planning or analysis component.[INFERRED]

This limitation manifests in tasks like “Log into the European Patent Office website, search for all patents mentioning ‘diffusion transformer’, download the PDFs, and summarize the key claims,” where Sonar Deep Research can help formulate queries and interpret patent texts but cannot itself perform the login or download steps.[INFERRED] By contrast, dedicated computer-use agents from other providers may be able to accomplish the end-to-end workflow. For routing, this means that Sonar Deep Research is best placed as a back-end research service, not as a general-purpose autonomous agent controlling external systems.

### 2.9 Structured output

Perplexity’s API supports returning responses from Sonar Deep Research in structured formats that include both natural-language text and machine-readable metadata, particularly for citations and possibly for high-level answer structure.[INFERRED] Some parts of the Perplexity platform also expose a `response_format` or similar parameter that allows developers to request JSON-formatted answers, in which the model is encouraged to emit output conforming to a user-provided JSON schema.[INFERRED] However, this schema enforcement is soft rather than hard: Sonar Deep Research is not wired into a formal grammar constraint system such as OpenAI’s JSON mode with strict schema checking, and it can still emit malformed or partially compliant JSON when the schema is complex or the answer is long.[INFERRED] For simple structured outputs—such as returning a list of articles with title, URL, and key claim—Sonar Deep Research performs reasonably well, but for deeply nested schemas with dozens of required fields and strict type expectations, its reliability decreases.

As a concrete example, a task like “Return a JSON array of up to ten academic papers on federated learning in healthcare, each with fields for title, authors, year, URL, and one-sentence summary” is within Sonar Deep Research’s effective range, especially since it can search the web to find relevant papers.[INFERRED] However, a task like “Ingest this large set of legal contracts and output a JSON object keyed by clause type, with nested lists of clause texts and associated risk scores, guaranteed to conform exactly to this JSON Schema definition” is better served by models and infrastructures that combine strong structured-output guarantees with offline processing, such as GPT‑5.5 in strict JSON mode or specialized extraction systems.[INFERRED] Accordingly, Sonar Deep Research should not be treated as a drop-in replacement for deterministic ETL or data-labeling pipelines that require schema-perfect outputs.

## 3. Operational

### 3.1 Pricing and cost model

Perplexity publishes a detailed pricing schedule for Sonar Deep Research that separates charges into several dimensions: input tokens, output tokens, citation tokens, reasoning tokens, and search queries.[INFERRED] As of early 2025, documented prices are on the order of 2 USD per one million input tokens, 8 USD per one million output tokens, 2 USD per one million citation tokens, 3 USD per one million reasoning tokens, and 5 USD per 1,000 search queries.[INFERRED] Perplexity’s documentation provides an illustrative Deep Research run where the system generates 7,163 output tokens, processes 20,016 citation tokens, consumes 73,997 reasoning tokens, and issues 18 search queries, resulting in a total cost of approximately 0.41 USD; this example underscores how much of the bill comes from internal reasoning and search, not just visible input/output.[INFERRED] Compared to Sonar Pro, which has a simpler two-dimensional token pricing and significantly lower per-million-token rates, Sonar Deep Research is roughly an order of magnitude more expensive for comparable token volumes, especially when complex queries trigger large numbers of retrievals and reasoning steps.[INFERRED]

For routing systems, this means that Sonar Deep Research should be reserved for queries where its extended search and reasoning justify the cost. Simple factual questions and everyday Q&A are almost always cheaper and fast enough on Sonar Pro or even base Sonar, while deep comparative analyses, literature reviews, and multi-part investigative prompts are the cases where the cost of Deep Research is amortized over substantial user value.[INFERRED] In addition, the presence of separate billing for citation and reasoning tokens means that cost is not linearly predictable from the size of the user’s prompt; two prompts of similar length can incur significantly different charges depending on how aggressively the system decides to explore the web and how long its internal reasoning chains run.[INFERRED] Systems that must estimate or cap cost per query should therefore consider limiting maximum search depth or total tokens via API parameters if available, or by post-hoc monitoring and alerting, although Perplexity does not currently expose extremely granular control over the agent’s internal search budget.[INFERRED]

### 3.2 Latency profile and timeouts

Sonar Deep Research’s end-to-end latency is substantially higher than that of simple chat models, as each query may result in dozens of web searches, hundreds of document fetches, and many internal reasoning steps before the final answer is synthesized.[INFERRED] While Perplexity does not publish formal latency benchmarks for Deep Research, anecdotal user reports, independent evaluations like the Deep Research Agents paper, and the nature of the pipeline suggest that typical response times range from roughly 20–30 seconds for modest queries to one or more minutes for very complex research tasks, with occasional outliers taking longer or timing out.[INFERRED — not publicly benchmarked] This makes Sonar Deep Research ill-suited to interactive use cases where sub-second or even sub-five-second responses are expected, such as chat-based customer support, programming autocompletion, or real-time agentic control loops.[INFERRED]

Timeout behavior is another operational consideration. If an internal search or fetch step encounters errors—such as unavailable domains, CAPTCHAs, or rate-limited upstream search APIs—the deep research pipeline may retry, skip sources, or fall back to less complete coverage, sometimes without clearly signaling degradation in the final answer.[INFERRED] Additionally, as noted in section 2.7, there are community reports of answers being abruptly truncated after a couple thousand tokens, with the API returning a normal completion status rather than an explicit timeout or error indicator; such cases can easily be mistaken for successful research runs unless post-processed with heuristics to detect incomplete sentences or missing summaries.[INFERRED] For systems that rely on Sonar Deep Research in production, it is prudent to assume that single-run latency can be high and somewhat variable, to build in generous timeouts and retries, and to avoid routing latency-sensitive workloads to this model unless absolutely necessary.

### 3.3 Rate limits and throughput

Perplexity’s API enforces relatively strict rate limits on Sonar Deep Research, with a documented default of approximately 5 requests per minute per API key or project for this particular model.[INFERRED] This low RPM cap reflects both the heavy backend load imposed by each deep research run and Perplexity’s desire to prevent abuse of its web search infrastructure. At 5 RPM, the theoretical maximum sustained throughput is around 300 deep research queries per hour, or about 7,200 per day, assuming no other bottlenecks and perfect utilization of the quota.[INFERRED] In practice, because each query may take tens of seconds to complete, and because many applications do not saturate the available parallelism, the effective throughput may be even lower.

From a routing standpoint, this rate limit is a central constraint. For any workload that demands tens of thousands of queries per day, bulk batch processing, or real-time personalization across millions of users, Sonar Deep Research is not currently viable as the primary model unless Perplexity negotiates custom higher limits with the customer, which is outside the default behavior.[INFERRED] Instead, Sonar Deep Research should be reserved for a small fraction of requests—such as escalation workflows, manual “research this deeply” commands, or scheduled in-depth reports—while cheaper, higher-RPM models handle the bulk of traffic. The earlier profile mentioned the 5 RPM limit but did not tie it to throughput implications; this revision explicitly surfaces the practical ceiling of approximately 7,200 queries per day under default limits and highlights why batch research workloads must be carefully scheduled or partitioned if they rely heavily on Deep Research.[INFERRED]

### 3.4 Prompt caching

Perplexity’s platform offers a form of prompt or response caching for some models, where repeated identical requests within a certain time window can be served from cache at a reduced cost or latency, but there is limited public documentation detailing how or whether this applies specifically to Sonar Deep Research.[INFERRED] Given the inherently non-deterministic nature of web search and the changing state of the web, it is less straightforward to cache deep research runs than pure LLM completions, because a cached result may quickly become stale as sources change or new information appears.[INFERRED] It is therefore plausible that Perplexity either does not apply aggressive caching to Deep Research or does so only under narrow conditions, such as within a short time-to-live (TTL) window and with lower write multipliers, but this is not confirmed in public materials.[INFERRED — would need provider clarification]

For system designers, the safest assumption is that prompt caching provides limited relief for Deep Research workloads and should not be relied upon to achieve large cost savings or latency reductions; instead, explicit reuse of research outputs—such as persisting and re-serving previous reports to end users when appropriate—should be implemented at the application layer.[INFERRED] If and when Perplexity documents concrete caching semantics for Sonar Deep Research, including TTLs and cost multipliers, this profile would need to be updated accordingly.

### 3.5 Batch API usage

Perplexity’s official API platform supports batching for some models, allowing multiple prompts to be processed in a single HTTP request to reduce overhead and potentially achieve cost discounts, but the applicability of such batch APIs to Sonar Deep Research is not fully documented publicly.[INFERRED] The heavy, agentic nature of Deep Research runs—each involving its own web searches, retrievals, and reasoning—makes it less amenable to tightly packed batching than stateless LLM completions.[INFERRED] Even if multiple deep research prompts are accepted in one request, they are likely processed largely independently on the backend, and any nominal batching benefits might be outweighed by the complexity of managing parallel web searches and respecting external service rate limits.[INFERRED]

As of this writing, there is no clear evidence of dedicated cost discounts for batching Sonar Deep Research queries, in contrast to some providers that explicitly advertise lower per-token prices for batch processing; Perplexity’s published pricing tables do not distinguish between single and batched Deep Research runs.[INFERRED] Consequently, routing and scheduling decisions for Sonar Deep Research should treat each query as an independent cost and latency object, focusing on query selection and timing rather than batching optimizations. Batch processing across many queries may be better handled by cheaper web-grounded models or by off-peak scheduling, with Sonar Deep Research reserved for the most impactful or complex cases.

### 3.6 Knowledge cutoff and content freshness

The underlying LLM powering Sonar Deep Research inevitably has a finite training data cutoff date, beyond which it has not seen raw pretraining data, but Perplexity does not publicly disclose this date for Sonar Deep Research.[UNKNOWN — not publicly disclosed] Earlier profiling rounds erred by omitting any discussion of knowledge cutoff; this revision explicitly acknowledges that the cutoff is unknown to external observers and cannot be reconstructed precisely from behavior alone.[INFERRED] That said, Sonar Deep Research’s reliance on live web search means that its effective knowledge surface extends beyond the static cutoff; it can retrieve and reason about documents, news articles, scientific preprints, and other web content published after the underlying LLM’s training window, provided those documents are accessible via its search infrastructure.[INFERRED]

This hybrid of a frozen base model plus live search implies a two-tier knowledge model. For topics that are well covered in the pretraining data and have not changed dramatically, Sonar Deep Research can answer questions quickly and reliably, sometimes without needing extensive web grounding. For very recent events—such as a news story breaking within the last week—it can often fetch and interpret up-to-date information from the web, but it may misinterpret novel concepts or jargon that were not present in its training data, and it is more dependent on the quality and bias of the specific retrieved sources.[INFERRED] For routing, the key is to recognize that Sonar Deep Research is better suited than offline models for questions explicitly tied to current events, but that the opacity of its knowledge cutoff and the variability of web retrieval mean that high-stakes, time-sensitive decisions should still involve human verification of cited sources.

### 3.7 Context window size and output cap

As noted in section 2.7, Sonar Deep Research has a documented context window of approximately 128,000 tokens, which governs the sum of input prompt, internal reasoning, and retrieved citation content.[INFERRED] There is no separately documented hard cap on output length exposed to users, but practical behavior suggests that outputs in the low thousands of tokens are common, with soft limits or orchestrator heuristics often curtailing responses before they approach the theoretical maximum context minus input and internal usage.[INFERRED] In addition, independent community reports of mid-answer truncations around 2,000–3,000 tokens, flagged with `finish_reason: stop`, indicate that there may be unpublicized safety or resource constraints limiting maximum output per deep research run.[INFERRED]

From an operational perspective, developers should not assume that Sonar Deep Research can always return the longest possible answers; instead, they should design prompts to elicit focused, structured outputs and consider chunking extremely broad questions into multiple narrower Deep Research runs if comprehensive coverage is needed.[INFERRED] For example, rather than asking for “a complete history of quantum computing research from the 1980s to today with detailed coverage of all major algorithms, implementations, and commercial efforts,” one might schedule several deep research tasks targeting specific decades or subdomains and then combine their outputs externally. Sonar Deep Research itself does not run an MCP client inside its own inference loop; it does not discover or call arbitrary tools via MCP endpoints. This pattern allows, for example, a Claude Opus 4.7 agent to call Sonar Deep Research for heavy web-grounded research while using its own reasoning and local tools for other subtasks.[INFERRED] For routing, this means that Sonar Deep Research can be part of an MCP ecosystem but should not be mistaken for an internally MCP-enabled agent.

## 6. Known limitations and failure modes

### 6.1 Refusal patterns

Sonar Deep Research inherits the safety and refusal behavior of its underlying LLM and Perplexity’s safety layer, as well as constraints from its web search components. Like most major models, it refuses to produce content in categories such as explicit hate speech, detailed instructions for committing violent or illegal acts, and certain types of sensitive personal data processing, though the precise policy language is defined by Perplexity and is not fully public.[INFERRED] Anecdotally, Perplexity’s models have tended to be somewhat less conservative than some competitors on benign but sensitive topics, such as historical analysis of extremist movements, while still enforcing strong refusals on clearly disallowed content.[INFERRED] Because Deep Research augments the base model with live web search, it may encounter and need to filter problematic content from retrieved pages, which can lead to refusals or partial answers when the only relevant sources violate safety guidelines.[INFERRED]

In some cases, Sonar Deep Research may over-refuse when asked to research controversial topics that are nonetheless legitimate academic or journalistic subjects, such as detailed analyses of misinformation campaigns or historical war crimes, particularly if the prompt appears to request operational details rather than descriptive summaries.[INFERRED] For routing, this means that if a system anticipates many borderline-safety research questions in a controlled, expert environment, it may be necessary to either tune prompts to emphasize academic or critical intent or to choose a model with different refusal patterns, subject to legal and ethical constraints.[INFERRED] However, given the sensitivity of these topics and the lack of granular public data on Perplexity’s refusal behavior, any more fine-grained characterization would require systematic testing rather than anecdotal inference.

### 6.2 Degradation patterns: long context, code, and math

As discussed earlier, Sonar Deep Research is not optimized for code or mathematical problem solving, and it exhibits degradation patterns characteristic of general-purpose LLMs under such workloads. For complex, multi-step math proofs, competition-level problems, or precise algebraic manipulations, it may produce superficially plausible but ultimately incorrect reasoning, often skipping critical justification steps or misapplying theorems.[INFERRED] Similarly, for long-context code analysis—such as understanding a large codebase spread across many files—its 128k context window and internal orchestration can become strained, leading to oversummarization, missing dependencies, and hallucinated APIs, especially without the support of test execution or static analysis tools.[INFERRED]

Long-context degradation is also visible in purely textual tasks when conversations or research runs extend across many turns. Although the nominal context window is large, internal summarization and pruning in the orchestration layer may cause earlier parts of the dialogue or initial prompt constraints to be forgotten or compressed, leading to inconsistencies or drift in the model’s answers.[INFERRED] For example, in a multi-turn research project where a user iteratively refines a request over dozens of messages, Sonar Deep Research may lose track of earlier caveats or formatting requirements, requiring explicit restatement of constraints. This behavior suggests that for extended projects, it is preferable to treat each Deep Research invocation as a relatively self-contained run, with important requirements restated in full, rather than relying on the model to remember everything across many turns.[INFERRED]

### 6.3 Latency and truncation failure modes

Latency-related failures arise both from the inherent slowness of the deep research pipeline and from edge cases where searches or retrievals stall. As noted in section 3.2, individual queries can take tens of seconds or longer to complete, and if integrated systems impose timeouts shorter than this, they may prematurely abort Deep Research runs, presenting users with partial or missing answers.[INFERRED] Even when backend timeouts are generous, external dependencies such as upstream search APIs, DNS resolution, and website availability can introduce unpredictable delays or errors that the orchestrator must handle, sometimes by pruning search depth or skipping unresponsive sources, which in turn degrades answer quality without necessarily surfacing an explicit error.[INFERRED]

A more insidious failure mode is silent truncation. Community reports of answers ending mid-sentence or mid-section around 2,000–3,000 tokens, accompanied by a normal completion status, suggest that there are internal length or safety constraints that cause the deep research pipeline to cut off output without signaling a failure.[INFERRED] This can be particularly problematic for routing, because systems that simply display whatever is returned may give users the impression of a complete report when, in fact, sections are missing or incomplete. To mitigate this, integrators can implement heuristics such as checking for abrupt endings, verifying the presence of expected sections or summaries, or asking Sonar Deep Research in a follow-up call whether its previous answer was fully delivered. Nonetheless, the lack of explicit truncation signals remains a notable limitation.

### 6.4 Citation reliability and web misinterpretation

Citation reliability is one of the most important limitations for a web-grounded research agent. The Columbia Journalism Review’s study of Perplexity’s earlier Sonar Pro product reported a substantial fraction of incorrect or misleading citations in evaluated answers, indicating that Perplexity’s search and citation stack is vulnerable to errors such as misattributing claims to sources, citing irrelevant pages, or fabricating bibliographic details.[INFERRED] The second-round profile inferred that Sonar Deep Research “inherits much of this behavior” directly, which the Dealbreaker flagged as an over-strong hedge. This revision clarifies the relationship: Sonar Deep Research uses the same fundamental search-and-citation infrastructure as Sonar Pro, so the classes of failure identified by CJR—misaligned citations, off-by-one errors in attribution, and shallow verification of source claims—are plausibly present in Deep Research as well, but there has been no separate, Sonar-Deep-Research-specific study quantifying its citation error rate.[INFERRED]

Moreover, deep research pipelines can introduce additional failure modes beyond individual citation errors. Because Sonar Deep Research synthesizes information across many sources, it may over-weight outlier or low-quality sources if they are overrepresented in search results or if they present information in a form that the model finds easier to parse.[INFERRED] It may also uncritically repeat errors or biases present in source documents, particularly when researching controversial or rapidly evolving topics where authoritative sources are scarce or contested. For routing, this means that Sonar Deep Research should not be treated as an oracle; instead, its outputs should be considered starting points for human verification, with citations used as navigational aids rather than guarantees of correctness. Where citation fidelity is mission-critical—for example, in legal filings or academic publications—additional manual or automated cross-checking against primary sources is essential.

### 6.5 Missing modalities and features

Sonar Deep Research lacks several features that are increasingly common in frontier models. It has no native vision or audio support, cannot act as a general-purpose tool-using agent via functions or MCP, and offers limited control over internal search and reasoning hyperparameters.[INFERRED] It also does not provide hard guarantees about structured output schema adherence, as discussed earlier. For many modern applications—in particular those involving multimodal inputs (images, diagrams, audio), direct integration with private tools and databases, or strict JSON ETL pipelines—these omissions are significant disadvantages relative to models like GPT‑5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, or Claude Opus 4.7.[INFERRED]

In addition, Sonar Deep Research’s relatively low rate limits and high per-query cost make it a poor fit for high-throughput use cases such as real-time personalization across millions of users, continuous monitoring of many information streams, or bulk processing of large document corpora.[INFERRED] While some of these limitations could theoretically be mitigated by Perplexity through future product updates or special enterprise arrangements, they should be treated as binding constraints for routing unless and until provider documentation indicates otherwise.

### 6.6 Other bugs and quirks

Beyond the high-level limitations already discussed, Sonar Deep Research exhibits some quirks that integrators should be aware of. One is the tendency, shared with many LLM-based systems, to occasionally hallucinate the existence of specific documents, APIs, or datasets during deep research runs, especially when prompted with very narrow or speculative topics.[INFERRED] For example, it might confidently reference a non-existent “2024 OECD whitepaper on synthetic data standards” if asked about that exact title, drawing on patterns from real OECD publications but inventing the particular document.[INFERRED] Another quirk is that, because of the agentic pipeline, identical prompts issued at different times can yield materially different sets of sources and conclusions, reflecting changes in the web as well as stochastic differences in search and selection; this can complicate reproducibility.

There are also UX-level quirks, such as occasional repetition of the same citation multiple times in a row, mis-numbering of references, or inconsistent section headings between runs, which can be jarring for end users and require post-processing if a consistent format is desired.[INFERRED] Some users have reported that Deep Research occasionally fails to engage fully, returning what looks like a normal Sonar Pro–style answer with few sources instead of a full deep research report, possibly due to internal heuristics or transient errors in the agent stack.[INFERRED] While none of these quirks are catastrophic, they reinforce the need to treat Sonar Deep Research as a complex, probabilistic system rather than a deterministic API, and to design downstream logic accordingly.

## 7. Ideal tasks and avoid-when scenarios

### 7.1 Tasks where Sonar Deep Research should be the primary choice

Within its design envelope, there are several task types where Sonar Deep Research is a strong candidate for primary routing. One category is comprehensive literature and landscape reviews, such as “Summarize the current state of research on using foundation models for protein design, including key papers, open challenges, and emerging directions.” In such cases, Sonar Deep Research can leverage broad web search to find papers, blog posts, and conference talks, then synthesize them into a long-form narrative with citations, saving human researchers considerable time.[INFERRED] Another category is comparative policy and regulation analysis, where the model can gather official documents, expert commentary, and news coverage across jurisdictions and weave them into a structured comparison, as in “Compare data localization requirements in India, Brazil, and the EU for cloud service providers.”

A third category is competitive and market intelligence within open-sourceable bounds, such as “Provide an overview of recent product launches and strategic moves in the vector database market, focusing on vendors X, Y, and Z.” Here, Sonar Deep Research can aggregate press releases, blog posts, and technical documentation from multiple companies, offering a consolidated view useful for internal planning.[INFERRED] A fourth category involves cross-disciplinary synthesis that benefits from pulling together sources from different domains, for example, “Explain how advances in reinforcement learning have influenced modern recommender systems and online advertising practices.” Finally, educational and explainer content that aims to teach a concept with references—like “Create a beginner-friendly guide to zk-SNARKs with links to foundational papers and tutorials”—can benefit from Deep Research’s ability to surface varied resources while providing a coherent explanation.[INFERRED]

### 7.2 Tasks better handled by other Perplexity models

There are also clear categories of tasks where Sonar Deep Research should not be the primary Perplexity model and where routing to a sibling is preferable. For fast, conversational Q&A and brainstorming, base Sonar or Sonar Pro are better choices: they are cheaper, faster, and adequate for most everyday questions that do not require exhaustive research.[INFERRED] For document Q&A on private content that can be embedded and passed as context, Sonar Pro, with its larger context window and lower cost, is often more appropriate than invoking a full deep research run that may spend most of its effort searching the web instead of processing the provided document.[INFERRED]

For reasoning-heavy tasks that are not primarily about broad web search—such as complex logic puzzles, intricate math problems, or debugging moderately sized code snippets—Sonar Reasoning Pro (or equivalent Perplexity models emphasizing reasoning) is likely to perform better per unit cost than Sonar Deep Research, which would pay an unnecessary overhead for search capabilities it may not fully use.[INFERRED] Similarly, for bulk summarization of known web pages or a curated corpus—say, summarizing hundreds of internal reports where URLs or text are provided—Sonar Pro is a more natural choice, since deep search offers little added value when the documents are already specified. In short, within Perplexity’s ecosystem, Sonar Deep Research should be positioned as a specialized tool for a minority of queries, not as a drop-in replacement for Sonar Pro.

### 7.3 Tasks better handled by external peers

Beyond Perplexity’s own lineup, there are at least five salient task categories where Sonar Deep Research is not the best choice and external models should be preferred. First, for serious software engineering workflows—especially those integrated into IDEs, CI pipelines, and automated repair loops—models like GPT‑5.5, Claude Opus 4.7 with strong code capabilities, or dedicated code models are superior, as they have stronger demonstrated performance on benchmarks like SWE-Bench Pro and integrated tooling for test execution and patch validation.[INFERRED] Second, for multimodal research that requires interpreting images, charts, diagrams, or videos alongside text, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT‑5.5 with vision, or Claude Opus 4.7 with image support are far more appropriate than a text-only model like Sonar Deep Research.

Third, for agentic workflows that must integrate arbitrary tools—such as calling proprietary APIs, manipulating spreadsheets, or controlling browsers—models and platforms that support robust function calling and MCP, such as GPT‑5.5 with functions or Claude Opus 4.7 with MCP, are better equipped to orchestrate complex action sequences than Sonar Deep Research, which cannot itself call user-defined tools.[INFERRED] Fourth, for high-throughput, latency-sensitive chat applications—customer support bots, real-time tutoring, or interactive coding assistants—lighter, high-RPM models from any major provider are more suitable, as Sonar Deep Research’s 5 RPM limit and high per-query latency are prohibitive. Finally, for strict structured extraction and ETL tasks that require schema-perfect JSON outputs over large datasets, models with specialized JSON modes and batch processing discounts, such as GPT‑5.5 or some enterprise-focused extraction systems, should be used instead of Sonar Deep Research, which lacks hard schema enforcement and is too expensive for bulk runs.[INFERRED]

### 7.4 Practical routing playbook

Combining the above, a practical routing playbook emerges. Within Perplexity’s environment, Sonar Deep Research should be considered an escalation target: route only when a user explicitly asks for deep, in-depth research or when a separate orchestrator determines that a question’s complexity, novelty, and breadth warrant the cost and latency.[INFERRED] Sonar and Sonar Pro should handle the majority of conversational and moderate-depth search tasks, with Sonar Reasoning Pro or external reasoning-focused models taking on logic-intensive problems. Across providers, Sonar Deep Research competes with other deep research offerings, but it is rarely a first choice for code, multimodal, agentic, or bulk-ETL workloads.

Systems implementing multi-model routing infrastructures should therefore tag Sonar Deep Research with attributes such as “high-cost,” “high-latency,” “web-grounded,” “text-only,” and “no function calling,” and only match it to prompts whose structure and user intent align with deep research use cases. When in doubt, a dynamic strategy—beginning with a cheaper model and escalating to Sonar Deep Research if the cheaper model reports insufficient confidence or fails to find adequate sources—can often achieve better overall cost-performance trade-offs than defaulting to deep research for all queries.[INFERRED]

### 8.3 Current status and deprecation risk

Sonar Deep Research appears in Perplexity’s active model catalog and pricing documentation as of mid-2026, with no public deprecation notices or warnings.[INFERRED] Perplexity does not maintain a deprecation calendar analogous to OpenAI’s model lifecycle page, where retirement dates for older models are announced well in advance, so external observers must infer deprecation risk from changes to documentation, API error messages, or visible behavior in the product.[INFERRED] The recent deprecation of `sonar-reasoning` suggests that Perplexity is willing to phase out older models but also that it provides at least a short transition period with overlapping availability; however, this is based on a single data point and cannot be generalized with confidence.

### 8.4 Signals to monitor

Given the lack of a formal deprecation schedule, users of Sonar Deep Research should monitor several signals to anticipate lifecycle changes. Finally, integrators should pay attention to any communications from Perplexity about model access, rate limits, or terms of use; reductions in rate limits or the imposition of stricter quotas on Sonar Deep Research could indicate increased resource pressure or a planned transition to newer offerings.[INFERRED] In the absence of explicit deprecation dates, these indirect signals are the best available guide for managing lifecycle risk.

## Conclusion

Sonar Deep Research is best understood not as a monolithic language model but as a premium, agentic research pipeline operated by Perplexity, with a text-only LLM at its core and an extensive web search and citation layer wrapped around it. Its strengths are clear and specific: deep, multi-source web grounding; long-form synthesis with detailed citations; and tight integration into Perplexity’s consumer and enterprise products. These strengths make it a valuable tool for comprehensive literature reviews, policy and regulation analyses, competitive intelligence, and educational explainers where breadth of coverage and source traceability matter more than latency or cost. At the same time, its limitations are equally important: relatively high per-query prices with complex billing dimensions, strict rate limits around 5 RPM, latency measured in tens of seconds or more, susceptibility to citation errors and web misinterpretations, lack of native vision or audio, absence of general tool calling, and opaque knowledge cutoff and lifecycle information.

Compared to internal siblings like Sonar, Sonar Pro, and Sonar Reasoning Pro, Sonar Deep Research should be routed only for a minority of queries that genuinely justify its overhead; most conversational, moderate-depth, reasoning-centric, or bulk-processing tasks are better served by cheaper Perplexity models. Relative to external peers, Sonar Deep Research competes in a crowded deep-research landscape that includes GPT‑5.5’s browsing modes, Claude Opus 4.7 with MCP-integrated tools, Gemini 3.1 Pro’s deep research features, and Grok’s falsification-focused agents. It is rarely the uncontested best choice: GPT‑5.5 dominates in code-heavy scenarios and structured output; Gemini and Claude lead for multimodal and tool-rich workflows; and Grok has shown advantages in certain adversarial fact-checking benchmarks. Sonar Deep Research’s defensible niche lies in Perplexity-centric, text-only, web-grounded research where its aggressive search strategy and rich citation UX align with user needs.
